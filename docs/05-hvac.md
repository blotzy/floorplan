# HVAC and ventilation

[← Back to README](../README.md)

Two ducted heat pump systems, ideally sharing one multi-zone outdoor unit: a full-size air handler for the great room and the bedroom wing, and a slim ducted unit for the primary suite alone, each with its own thermostat; no motorized zone dampers. Plus a continuously running exhaust fan for code-required fresh air. Every room gets ceiling registers. Capacities are rough; the HVAC contractor's Manual J sets the final sizes. No electric backup heat strips are needed in this climate.

| System | Serves | Thermostat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main | Great room (kitchen, dining, lounge, foyer), gym, guest, office, guest ensuite, bedroom hall | Great room, on an interior wall away from the dining glass and the range |
| Primary suite | Primary bedroom, bath, dressing room | Primary bedroom |

Fallbacks, if the equipment or the budget changes: if the chosen equipment can't run both indoor units from one outdoor unit, the suite gets its own small outdoor unit beside the main one; if the suite's system is dropped, one variable-speed system with two zones on motorized dampers (day: the great room, office, gym and guest; night: the primary suite), with a Manual D showing the suite zone can take the unit's minimum airflow without noise.

**Heat pumps**

- Outdoor unit: one multi-zone heat pump (about 3 tons in all) on a noncombustible pad in the rear yard against the south wall behind the storage room (no windows on that stretch; short line sets into the attic), with a dog guard. Leave room beside it for a second, small outdoor unit (the fallback). See [site](03-site-and-landscape.md).
- Main air handler in the sealed attic, near the attic hatch, over the primary dressing room (short line set to the outdoor unit, condensate drain near the laundry's drains), on a service platform with a light and outlet. Secondary drain pan with a float switch under it; condensate drain route planned before drywall.
- Primary suite unit: a slim ducted indoor unit on the same platform beside the main air handler, with short ducts to the primary bedroom, bath and dressing room and its own return from the primary bedroom, filtered at the unit (the best filter its fan allows). Its own drain pan with a float switch, draining with the main unit's condensate.
- Two thermostats, no bypass duct. Size the main system's ducts so the unit runs quietly at its minimum airflow; the contractor confirms both systems with a Manual D duct design. Manual balancing dampers on each branch.
- Central MERV 13 filter on the main return at the air handler (media cabinet), for wildfire smoke; easy to reach for changes.
- Returns: a central return in the bedroom hall and one in the great room; transfer grilles or jumper ducts from each closed-door room (guest, office, gym). The primary suite's return goes to its own unit.
- Supplies: ceiling registers in the flat-ceiling rooms, near the windows and throwing across the room. The vaulted great room has no attic above it, so its supplies are high sidewall registers on the walls bordering the attic (the lounge/gym and kitchen/pantry walls), throwing across the room, with ducting in the insulated rafter bays only where unavoidable. The dining room's large south glass gets its own supply; the great room's return sits low on one of those walls. Destratification (a ceiling fan) helps under the high clerestory.
- Gym: keep registers out of the ring swing zone.
- Smoke alarms sit on the ceiling just inside each bedroom door (sheet A-2): keep supply registers at least 3' away and not blowing at them.
- Primary bedroom register aimed along the room, not at the bed.
- Primary, guest and office have ceiling fans: keep their registers out from under the blades and don't aim them at the fans.
- No return in the kitchen (grease) or the laundry (lint).
- Ducts stay in the sealed attic; no ducts, returns, or vent openings into the carport.
- Attic duct layout around the storage (decked areas on sheet A-7: the gym zone across the hall under the shed, and the strip along the gable's ridge over the hall): nothing crosses or runs under the decking. Two supply trunks along the ridge strip's long edges, one for the street-side rooms and one for the rear-side rooms, with each branch running outward through the low attic toward the street and rear walls, where the attic is too low for storage anyway. The great room's supplies run along the low rear edge of the shed attic, not across the gym storage. The hall return sits by the air handler so its duct stays short and out of the strip. The primary suite unit's ducts stay over the suite, west of the platform. Bath fan and fresh-air exhaust ducts also run outward to their terminations. Keep the walkway from the hatch to the air handler, and a path down the middle of the decking, clear. The Manual D duct design follows this layout.

**Ventilation**

- Whole-house ventilation: a quiet (1.0 sone or less), continuous-rated exhaust fan running all the time at the code's ventilation rate, in the common bath ceiling (central to the house), doubling as that bath's exhaust fan with a humidity boost, through its own ember-resistant roof cap with a damper. Pick one of the quietest continuous-rated models (about 0.3-0.5 sone), since the bath opens onto the bedroom hall. Outdoor air makes up through the envelope (exhaust-only); an ERV, if added, replaces it. The other bath fans run on demand (humidity or timer switches). No laundry fan or dryer vent (ventless heat pump dryer). Terminations: the range hood and the guest ensuite fan through the roof (the ensuite's on the wing's street slope), the common bath's (the continuous fan) through its own roof cap (separate from its skylight), and the primary bath's straight up from the bath through its own roof cap (a short vertical duct, staying in the low strip of attic by the rear wall). No wall caps.
- Range hood ducted outside through the roof, 400 CFM or less to avoid a makeup-air requirement.
- Network rack and water heater share the mechanical closet, which opens into the laundry through a louvered 36" bifold so the water heater can draw on the laundry's air (and the rack's waste heat); no conditioned supply into the closet.
